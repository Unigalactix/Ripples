I think `this concept` has a strong emotional core and a clear dramatic shape. The central conflict is relatable: **one person wants their pain witnessed, while the other expresses love by trying to remove it**. Neither person has to be the villain, which gives the story maturity.

## What works especially well

### 1. The conflict is emotionally credible

Mukund is not indifferent. He researches venues, proposes alternatives, and tries to restore Aarna’s future. His mistake is responding to the practical problem before acknowledging the emotional loss.

Aarna is not necessarily asking him to solve anything. She wants permission to collapse safely beside someone she trusts. The line:

> “I didn’t come to you to be strong. I came to you because I need to be weak right now…”

expresses that need clearly and could become the story’s defining line.

The deeper theme is therefore not simply “emotionally available versus unavailable.” It is:

> **What happens when someone offers solutions as love, but their partner experiences those solutions as abandonment?**

That is more nuanced and less accusatory.

### 2. The five-wave structure fits the title

The movement from impact, to widening consequences, to confrontation, vulnerability, and stillness is elegant. It also gives you a natural way to reveal their three-year history without stopping the present-day story for a large block of exposition.

The best version would make each memory answer a question raised in the present:

- Why does Aarna hear “be strong” as rejection?
- Why does Mukund experience helplessness as failure?
- When has his practicality helped her before?
- When has she previously failed to recognize his way of loving?
- Why has this conflict remained unresolved for three years?

That would make the past feel causally connected rather than inserted merely to explain the characters.

### 3. The ending avoids an unrealistic transformation

It is good that Mukund does not become perfectly emotionally articulate overnight. A believable resolution would be one small act demonstrating that he finally understands what she is asking for.

For example, he could close the laptop, sit on the floor, and ask:

> “Do you want me to help, or do you want me to stay?”

That is a small behavioral change, but it carries more weight than a long declaration.

## What needs strengthening

### 1. The reason for the cancellation conflicts with Aarna’s interpretation

The gallery withdraws because of funding or management changes, but Aarna later says:

> “They rejected me.”

Objectively, they did not reject her art. She can certainly *feel* rejected, but the story should acknowledge that distinction.

You have two good options:

- Keep the funding cancellation and show that Aarna irrationally internalizes an impersonal setback.
- Make the rejection more personal—for example, new management considers the collection too difficult or commercially risky.

The first option is psychologically richer because Mukund can be factually correct while still being emotionally wrong in that moment.

### 2. Mukund is currently a little too perfectly engineered around the theme

His profession, vocabulary, clothing, worldview, and proposed solutions all point toward the same trait. That makes him immediately understandable, but possibly too schematic—as though his career exists solely to explain his emotional limitation.

Medical sales can remain his profession, but I would make its influence subtler. Instead of having him explicitly compare Aarna’s paintings to medical inventory, show his habits:

- He opens his laptop without asking.
- He begins making a list of alternative venues.
- He calls someone while Aarna is still speaking.
- He uses phrases such as “next steps” and “contingency.”
- Silence visibly makes him uncomfortable.

Those behaviors reveal him more naturally than a speech explaining his professional philosophy.

It would also help to give his fixing instinct a personal origin beyond his career. Perhaps he grew up in a home where distress had consequences, where nobody came to comfort him, or where being useful was the only reliable way to receive affection. Then his behavior becomes a survival language, not merely a salesperson stereotype.

### 3. Aarna needs complexity beyond being emotionally correct

At present, the outline largely positions Aarna as the person who correctly understands emotional intimacy and Mukund as the person who must learn it. That risks making the dual perspective unequal.

Give Aarna a genuine blind spot as well. For example:

- She expects Mukund to intuit her needs rather than stating them.
- She dismisses practical help even when it later becomes necessary.
- She interprets his discomfort as lack of love.
- She has previously depended on his problem-solving but resents it now.
- She asks for vulnerability while making it unsafe for him to reveal his own limitations.

Her realization should be more than “he loves differently.” She might recognize that she has been measuring his love exclusively in her own emotional language.

This does **not** mean both characters must be equally at fault in this incident. It means both should have something meaningful to learn.

### 4. The same-night commitment could feel too tidy

A three-year relational pattern probably cannot be resolved in a few hours. It can, however, be **recognized** in one day.

The ending will feel more truthful if they commit to a concrete practice rather than claiming the problem is solved:

- Mukund will ask what kind of support she wants before acting.
- Aarna will state whether she wants comfort, advice, or practical help.
- They will return to the exhibition problem the next morning.
- Mukund will sit with her that night without reopening the laptop.

The resolution should be a new beginning, not proof that they are healed.

### 5. Some symbolism may become too explicit

“Ripples,” five named waves, paintings as pieces of her soul, Mukund selling devices that physically fix people, and the repeated language of fixing all reinforce the same idea. Individually they work, but together they may make the story feel designed rather than lived.

I would keep the wave structure as an underlying architecture but avoid announcing every metaphor within the prose. Let readers feel the ripple effect rather than repeatedly explaining it.

## A stronger emotional progression

The five waves could move like this:

1. **Aarna receives the cancellation.** Mukund arrives and immediately begins helping.
2. **His competence intensifies her loneliness.** He interprets her resistance as surrender or self-sabotage.
3. **Their historical pattern surfaces.** She accuses him of never staying with her pain; he reveals that watching her suffer makes him feel useless and frightened.
4. **They separate briefly.** Each recognizes the injury beneath the other’s behavior.
5. **He returns without a plan.** She explicitly asks him to sit with her. Practical decisions are postponed until morning.

That final postponement is important. Mukund does not have to renounce problem-solving; he learns that **timing is part of empathy**.
