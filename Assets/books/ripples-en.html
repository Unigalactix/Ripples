Dedication

To every pebble I have faced in my life and every ripple I have managed. To the still water that follows every ripple, and to the pebbles that will create ripples in the days to come.

This book is dedicated to you all.

At the Water's Edge

Before

First draft

Mukund

At six on Friday evening, Mukund came home carrying a book he had not opened all day.

He left his car keys beside Aarna's in the bowl by the door. Beyond the living-room windows, a boat moved between the reflected buildings on the Chicago River. The broken lengths of light joined again behind it.

"Did you eat?" she called from her workroom.

"We're going to dinner."

"That wasn't the question."

He had bought a sandwich between hospital appointments and forgotten it in the passenger seat. He decided to remember it later.

Their apartment had three bedrooms. They slept in one; the other two had become their workspaces when they moved in together during their second year. His room contained a desk, product samples and books stacked behind books. Hers had a table she called temporarily covered, although he had not seen its surface in months.

He put his book on the broad windowsill beside the chair where he usually read. Aarna's sketchbook was open on the opposite end. She had drawn the same doorway three times and crossed out none of them.

They were supposed to be celebrating tonight. The gallery had accepted her collection weeks ago, but finding an evening without a late appointment or another piece to finish had become its own small project. He had finally made a reservation at the place she kept looking at when they walked past.

"Seven-thirty," he said. "We don't have to leave yet."

She appeared holding two shirts.

"Which one?"

"For me or you?"

"Mukund."

"The blue."

"You say that about everything."

"It's a consistent position."

She put the other shirt over his book.

They mostly ate out. On weekends they sometimes cooked together, moving around each other in a kitchen that felt large until both opened a cupboard. He cut things too carefully; she used every spoon. Last Sunday they had eaten standing up because neither had cleared the table.

His mother had sent a message from San Jose asking him to listen to the introduction for her next podcast. She interviewed celebrities and other prominent people around the country, mostly Indians who had settled in the United States. He had promised to listen after work.

He sent, "Dinner tonight. Tomorrow morning?"

Her reply was a thumbs-up, followed by a correction to her own previous message. His elder brother, a software engineer who also lived in San Jose, had already found a fault with the recording volume.

Mukund put the phone down and hummed the beginning of an old film song. Aarna joined him for three words, then stopped when he found his usual wrong note.

"Don't sing that in front of my father."

"He offered me a discount on a keyboard."

"He was trying to help."

Aarna

She took the blue shirt to their bedroom and returned for the other one. Mukund had uncovered his book but kept her place in the sketchbook with an unopened envelope.

Three years together, and he still thought anything left open might need protecting from a draft.

They had met through B(L)IND, an app that arranged blind dates with random people. At a cafe in Chicago, she had checked the table number twice before sitting down. He asked about the paint on her wrist instead of asking what she did for a living.

She told him about a room whose wall changed colour after sunset. He asked what colour it became. She tried three answers. He stirred a cup that no longer needed stirring and waited for the third.

His family, like hers, had come from India and settled in America. Her parents were in New Jersey, where they owned a musical-instrument shop. She had graduated from college in New York state. He had graduated in California and moved to Chicago for work. When he said back home, he still sometimes meant San Jose.

They both had previous relationships. They talked about them early, without making each other compete with people who were no longer there, and left them in the past.

For a year, Mukund had driven her almost everywhere: to work, to buy supplies, to dinners they had been too tired to cook. Now they each had a car. Sometimes, when she finished late, she still checked her phone for the message saying he was downstairs.

Once he had waited outside an art-supply store while she went in for one brush. She returned with a roll of paper too long for the car. They spent ten minutes folding the passenger seat down, arguing about whether diagonally counted as a solution.

On the drive home the roll lay between them. At a red light he reached around it to offer her the last fry.

They still argued about which movie to see. Hindi, English, Telugu: neither language nor subtitles settled the question of whether something was worth leaving the apartment for. On nights they could not agree, they walked along the river and listened to songs through one earbud each.

She looked at him now, turning to the first page of his book although he had less than an hour.

"We're not taking that to dinner."

"I'm checking whether it's good."

"And if it is?"

"Difficult evening for you."

She touched the back of his neck as she passed.

Room Enough was leaning against the living-room wall, brought out of her workroom so she could see it from farther away. A blue room, a pale doorway, three marks from a coat hook. Two weeks until the exhibition. She had meant to photograph it before they left.

Her phone lit up beside the frame.

A Pebble

First draft

Aarna

The gallery's name appeared above a paragraph beginning with an apology.

Aarna read the first sentence, then the last. The funding had fallen through. Her solo exhibition could not proceed. They could not offer another date.

In the window, the blue shirt she was holding reflected back at her.

"What's happened?"

She held up a finger. The coordinator was answering her call.

There was no anger in her voice at first. She asked whether this meant postponement. Whether the artists had been told before the decision. Whether there was anything she should do.

Across the room, Mukund put his book down.

"But the invitations have gone out," she said.

The coordinator apologised again. It was not a judgement on the paintings.

Aarna looked at Room Enough. She had spent a whole afternoon painting over one of the coat-hook marks, then another putting it back.

She said thank you before ending the call. She disliked that she had said it.

Mukund was standing beside her now.

"It's canceled."

"The show?"

She nodded.

He touched her shoulder. She let the shirt fall over the back of a chair and leaned into him.

"All of it?"

"All of it."

For several seconds he said nothing. His shirt smelled faintly of the car and the soap they both used.

Then she remembered dinner.

"Cancel the table."

"We could still go."

"Please, Mukund."

He picked up his phone.

While he called the restaurant, she opened the transport booking on hers. She could cancel without losing the deposit if she did it before Monday. She saved the confirmation in the folder with the other receipts.

Her hands knew what to do. The rest of her had not caught up.

Room Enough came from her first Chicago rental. She used to put her paints in a suitcase beneath the bed whenever someone visited. The handle stuck out unless she pushed it with her foot. In the painting the doorway was wider than it had ever been.

Her father had sent a photograph from New Jersey that afternoon: the exhibition invitation in the shop window, between a guitar and a sign about lessons. She opened it again.

He had used two pieces of tape to keep the paper straight.

She started typing, "Please take it down."

Then she locked the phone.

"It's canceled," Mukund said from the other side of the room.

For a second she thought he meant the exhibition.

"The reservation," he added.

She sat on the sofa. On his reading chair, the book had fallen shut.

The Ripples Begin

Wave 1

The First Ripple

First draft

Mukund

Mukund went into his workroom for the laptop.

He remembered a small upstairs space where they had seen photographs the previous winter. It had windows, white walls, a number on the door. Somewhere he still had the name.

When he returned, Aarna had taken her earrings off. They lay on the sofa cushion beside her.

"There are other rooms," he said.

She rubbed one ear without looking up.

"The work's finished. We don't have to start from nothing."

"Can you not do that yet?"

"I'm just looking."

He opened the laptop. She stood.

"Then look."

Her workroom door closed harder than it needed to.

He sat for a moment with his fingers above the keyboard. Then he searched for the upstairs space and wrote one message. He did not send it until he had read it three times.

When Aarna came back, she was wearing an old sweater. He closed the laptop halfway.

"You had anything since lunch?"

"No."

"Me neither."

That was when he remembered the sandwich in his car.

"Actually, I have a sandwich slowly becoming a problem downstairs."

She looked at him. The corner of her mouth moved, then settled.

"Let's go somewhere normal," she said.

He reached for his keys.

"We can walk."

He put them back beside hers.

Aarna

They went to a small place a few blocks away where nobody needed to know what they had meant to celebrate.

The server recognised them and asked whether they wanted their usual table.

Aarna said yes too quickly.

Mukund gave her the seat away from the door. He always did. She used to complain about the cold reaching her ankles; she could not remember the last time she had needed to.

For a while they discussed nothing except food.

He ordered something they could share and forgot that she had stopped liking it. She reminded him. He changed the order.

The server left.

"I thought you liked that."

"I did."

He nodded and unfolded his napkin.

She watched another couple arranging their plates for a photograph. The woman moved a water glass twice. The man waited with his fork in the air.

"My father put the invitation in the window."

Mukund looked up.

"At the shop?"

She showed him the photograph. He enlarged it with two fingers.

"He's put it right in the middle."

She nodded.

"Have you told him?"

"Not yet."

He gave the phone back.

The food arrived. She found she was hungry enough to resent the first few bites.

Mukund moved the hotter dish away from her sleeve. She passed him the napkins.

They had done these things in this restaurant on good evenings and on evenings when work had left them barely able to speak. Tonight the movements were the same. She kept waiting for that to make the evening familiar.

Mukund

His phone lit up with the reply from the venue.

He looked at the first line before he could stop himself.

"They might have a date," he said.

Aarna put down her fork.

"You sent something?"

"Just asked."

"I said not yet."

"I didn't promise them anything."

She looked towards the next table and lowered her voice.

"That's not the part I'm talking about."

He locked the phone and put it in his pocket.

The server came to ask whether everything was all right. They both said yes.

When she had gone, Aarna pushed the bread towards him.

"Eat," she said.

He tore off a piece. Neither of them said anything for the next few minutes.

Wave 2

Crosscurrents

First draft

Aarna

They walked home beside the river.

Mukund had the small paper bag of leftovers. She kept putting her hands into her coat pockets and taking them out again. The wind changed direction whenever they passed an opening between buildings.

Usually they chose a song before they reached the water. Tonight neither reached for an earbud.

"I know you were trying to help," she said.

"Okay."

"But when I say wait, I need you to wait."

"I put it away."

"After you sent the message."

He looked across the water.

"I heard you."

She stopped walking.

"Did you?"

A jogger passed between them and the railing. Mukund moved the bag to his other hand.

She knew that question had sounded worse than she meant it to. She also wanted an answer.

For a year he had waited outside work, outside stores, outside a friend's building while she went back for something she had forgotten. Sometimes she had come downstairs already apologising and found him reading, one finger keeping his place while he leaned over to unlock the door.

Those evenings had been easy to recognise as love.

"I didn't need another venue," she said.

"You might tomorrow."

"It's still today."

He looked at her then. For a moment she thought he was going to laugh, and the thought made her angrier.

He only nodded.

They began walking again.

Mukund

He wanted to say he had understood the first time.

But if he had understood, he was not sure why he had sent the message.

He watched the path ahead instead. Aarna walked near the river. He took the side nearer the bicycles without thinking about it.

"Do you want to sit?" he asked when they reached a bench.

She shook her head, then stopped.

They sat with the paper bag between them.

Someone on the opposite bank was taking a group photograph. A laugh crossed the water clearly enough that Mukund looked up.

"I wanted to wear the earrings my mother sent," Aarna said.

He waited.

"At the opening. She asked for a picture."

"You can still..."

She turned away.

"Sorry," he said.

A boat went under the bridge. He waited for it to emerge.

When Aarna stood, he picked up the bag. They walked the rest of the way without discussing the show.

At the building entrance she could not find her keys. He opened the door. In the elevator she took the bag from him because the handle was cutting into his fingers.

Upstairs, she put the leftovers in the refrigerator and moved a carton of milk so the door would close.

He stood in the kitchen, still wearing his coat.

She asked whether he had read the gallery's whole message.

He said yes.

She asked which part made him think she wanted to start planning again.

He took his coat off slowly.

Aarna

She could hear herself returning to it.

The thing she wanted was still somewhere on the other side of the same sentences, and every time she tried again he looked more tired.

"I'm not asking you to fix it," she said.

"I know."

"Then why do you keep..."

"Because I don't know what else to do, Aarna."

He had not shouted. His voice was louder than it had been all evening.

She stopped.

Mukund rarely lost his temper. When he did, it startled her more than her own anger startled her.

"You could start by not talking to me like that."

He looked at the coat in his hands.

"You're right. I shouldn't have raised my voice."

She wanted the apology to be enough. She wanted him to understand why it was not.

"But you're still angry with me."

He folded the coat over the chair.

"Yes," he said. "A little."

Wave 3

The Crest

First draft

Aarna

The river below their window was no longer blue. Light from the opposite buildings broke across it in narrow strips.

Aarna picked up Room Enough and carried it towards her workroom.

"You don't have to put it away," Mukund said.

"I want it out of here."

"It's your room too."

She set the painting down.

"I know whose room it is."

He closed his eyes briefly.

She had meant the room they were standing in. He might have meant the whole apartment. She no longer trusted herself to ask.

"I don't want you to decide when I should be over it," she said.

"I'm not."

"You've spent all evening..."

"Please don't go through the whole list again."

She stared at him.

He rubbed his forehead. "That came out wrong."

"Everything comes out wrong tonight."

He looked towards his workroom. The door was open; the small desk lamp was on.

"Maybe I'm not the person you need for this."

She heard the first part before the last two words arrived.

"What does that mean?"

"It means I don't know how to help."

"Then say that."

He pulled a chair out and sat down.

She stayed beside the painting. Her throat hurt from holding her voice steady.

"I came to you to be weak for a while," she said. "Not to be told how to get out of it."

He nodded without looking up.

She could not tell whether he had heard her or only wanted her to stop.

Mukund

At ten-forty, his brother called.

It was still early evening in California. Mukund almost let it ring. Then he saw the second call arriving before the first notification had disappeared.

"I need to answer this."

He stepped into his workroom but left the door open.

His brother spoke too carefully.

"Mom's had a mild heart attack," his brother said. "She's at the hospital in San Jose. I'm with her."

Mukund stood without remembering that he had been sitting.

"Is she awake?"

"Yes. She's being treated. They're keeping a close watch on her."

His brother repeated that she was stable. Mukund held on to that word, then asked him to say it again.

Mukund put his free hand on the desk. The edge of a paperback bent beneath his fingers.

"Send me the hospital. And the room. Whatever they give you."

When the call ended, Aarna was standing in the doorway.

"Your mother?"

He nodded.

"Is someone with her?"

"My brother. A mild heart attack. She's awake. They're treating her."

Saying it aloud did not make the words arrange themselves into anything he understood.

Aarna came a little closer.

"Do you want me here?"

He nodded again.

She stayed beside the desk while he called his brother back.

Aarna

For several minutes there was only the practical work of knowing where someone was.

She wrote down the hospital name when he said it. Found the charger he was looking for. Moved his book out from under his hand.

His brother called again. Their mother was stable for now and being monitored. He would stay with her. Mukund put the call on speaker so he would not have to repeat it afterwards.

There was a short silence after they hung up.

"I'm going," he said.

"I know."

He opened a flight search. She brought her laptop from her workroom and sat beside him.

"You don't have to come."

"Do you want me to?"

He stopped typing.

"Yes."

"Then put two."

They found seats on a flight out of Chicago on Saturday morning. By the time they had booked, his brother had sent another update: no change, still being watched, he would call if anything changed.

Mukund read it twice.

Aarna went to their bedroom and brought out both carry-on bags.

The blue shirt was still on the bed. She folded it once, then set it aside.

Her phone lit up with a message from her father asking how dinner had been.

She wrote that the exhibition had been canceled, that Mukund's mother was in hospital, and that they were going to California in the morning. She would call when she could.

She sat on the edge of the bed after sending it.

The show was still gone. She was still hurt. Through the open door she could hear Mukund asking his brother the same question for the third time.

She got up and packed his charger.

Wave 4

The Trough

First draft

Mukund

They slept for perhaps two hours.

Before dawn on Saturday, Aarna drove them to the airport in her car. His remained in the apartment garage. He had reached for his own keys, then stood holding them until she asked whether he wanted to keep checking his phone instead.

At a red light she placed a bottle of water in the cup holder beside him.

He had done that for her on so many mornings that he looked at the bottle before taking it.

His brother's latest message said their mother had managed some sleep. She was still being monitored. They would speak again before boarding.

The roads were nearly empty. Aarna did not put music on.

At the airport he opened his book without finding the bookmark. The same paragraph waited through three announcements.

Aarna returned with coffee and something to eat.

"I can't," he said.

"Keep it for later."

She put the wrapped food into his bag.

He looked at her face. She had not slept enough either. There was a little paint beside one fingernail, missed when she washed her hands.

"I'm sorry about your dinner."

"It was our dinner."

He nodded.

She sat close enough that their sleeves touched, but did not lean against him.

Aarna

On the flight to San Jose, she gave him the window seat.

Usually she wanted it. She liked watching the shapes of roads disappear into the ground. Today he had been looking through people all morning; she thought he might prefer something that did not look back.

His brother had spoken to them just before boarding. Their mother was stable, and he would arrange for them to get to the hospital after landing.

Mukund kept his phone in his hand until the crew asked everyone to prepare for departure.

When the aircraft rose, he gripped the armrest.

She put her hand beside his. After a moment he covered it.

They said nothing until the cabin had settled into the ordinary noises of a flight. A trolley latch. Someone opening a packet behind them. The soft complaint of a child who wanted the window shade up.

"You didn't have to do all this," he said.

"You asked me to come."

"I know."

She watched him worry the edge of his boarding pass.

"I wanted you here last night too," she said.

His hand went still.

"I was there."

She looked down.

He let out a breath.

"I know. Not what you meant."

The flight attendant stopped beside them to ask about drinks. They answered. Aarna waited while Mukund unfolded his tray table and moved his book twice to make room.

"We don't have to finish this now," she said.

"I don't want to pretend it didn't happen."

She nodded.

"Neither do I."

Mukund

He was afraid that if they spoke about the exhibition, something would happen at the hospital while he was thinking about the wrong thing.

He knew it made no sense. He checked the blank phone screen anyway.

"I can't fix any of this from here," he said.

Aarna did not answer immediately.

"No."

He waited for the rest. It did not come.

Below them the clouds were bright enough that he lowered the shade halfway.

"Last night," he said, "I wanted you to stop hurting so badly. And then I got angry because I couldn't make that happen."

"I know you weren't trying to hurt me."

"I still did."

She took a sip of water.

"I shouldn't keep coming at you until you say it exactly right," she said.

"I should have stopped when you asked me to wait."

He looked at the narrow space between their seats.

"And I shouldn't have raised my voice."

"No."

She turned towards him.

"I'm sorry for how I spoke too."

He nodded. Neither apology made him feel immediately better. He was relieved she had not tried to make it.

"Are you still upset with me?" he asked.

"Some of it. Are you?"

"Some."

She rested her hand on the armrest again.

"I'm not going anywhere."

He almost laughed at the literal truth of it, two people fastened into seats above the country. Instead he put his hand over hers.

"Me neither," he said.

The flight attendant asked whether he wanted anything else. He shook his head.

Aarna remembered the food in his bag and brought it out. This time he ate half.

Wave 5

Still Water

First draft

Aarna

By Saturday afternoon they were at the hospital in San Jose.

Mukund's brother met them near the entrance carrying two coffees he had forgotten to drink. He looked at Aarna, then at the bag on her shoulder.

"Thank you for coming."

"Of course."

She did not say anything about Chicago.

Their mother was awake when they were allowed in. She looked tired against the pillows, but she reached for Mukund before he had decided where to stand.

"You didn't listen to the introduction," she said.

"I know."

"It was too long anyway."

He bent his head over her hand. Aarna looked towards the window.

Later, a doctor spoke with them. His mother was doing well after treatment for the mild heart attack. She was comfortable and stable; they would continue monitoring her and discuss her recovery and follow-up care.

Mukund asked whether that meant she was all right.

"She's doing well," the doctor repeated.

He nodded, then looked at his brother, who nodded too.

By evening she had eaten and complained about the tea. When Mukund offered to find another cup, she told him to sit down for five minutes.

Aarna watched him take the chair.

When his mother grew sleepy, his brother stayed with her and sent them downstairs. He would let them know if she needed anything.

At the elevator, Mukund looked back once. His brother lifted a hand.

Mukund

At nine-thirty on Saturday night, California time, the hospital lobby was almost empty.

They sat on a bench near the glass entrance. A vending machine hummed behind them. Somewhere beyond the doors, a car waited with its lights on.

For the first time since the phone call, Mukund put his phone in his pocket without checking the screen again.

"She's okay," he said.

Aarna nodded. "She is."

He let his head rest against the wall. He could still hear his brother's voice from Friday night, although the words no longer fitted what they knew.

Aarna put their bag on the floor and stretched her fingers. He had not noticed that she had been carrying it.

"Give me that."

"It's already down."

He looked at her. She smiled a little.

They sat quietly until her phone lit up. A message from her parents asking how his mother was doing. Aarna wrote back, then stopped at another message below it.

He recognised the gallery's name.

"You haven't had much room for that today."

"No."

She put the phone away.

"I'm glad your mother's okay. I am."

"I know."

"But I don't want yesterday to disappear because we got scared about something else."

He sat forward.

"It hasn't."

She looked at him, waiting.

"You asked me to stop planning. I kept going."

He rubbed his hands together.

"I should have stayed with you before I started looking for somewhere else to put the paintings."

"I shouldn't have kept coming at you like that," she said. "I knew I was doing it. I just wanted you to hear me."

"I did hear you. I didn't listen properly."

She looked down at the bag between their feet.

Aarna

Now that they were no longer waiting for bad news, she could feel how tired she was.

She had imagined this conversation several times since Chicago. In each version she had known exactly what to say. Here, with Mukund sitting beside her, all she could find was the part that had frightened her.

"When you said you might not be the person I needed..."

"I wasn't asking to leave."

"Let me finish."

He stopped.

"I know that now. But I need you not to put us there because you don't know what to do."

He nodded.

"I can say I don't know."

"You can."

A cleaner moved a cart past them. They drew their feet back. Aarna waited until the wheels had stopped rattling against the tiled floor.

"And if I need you to just sit with me, I'll say that," she said. "Not keep asking until you guess."

"I'll ask before I start making calls."

She nodded. He waited another moment.

"Are we okay?"

She looked at his face. The worry had not entirely left it, even with his mother well upstairs.

"Yes," she said. "We're okay."

He reached for her hand.

"The show still hurts."

"I know. That doesn't have to stop tonight."

She let her fingers settle between his.

His phone vibrated in his pocket. Their mother was asleep, his brother wrote. Resting comfortably. They should get some food and sleep themselves.

Mukund showed her the message.

She read it, then handed the phone back.

"Come here," she said.

He stood with her. For a second the bag was between them again; he moved it with his foot.

Aarna put her arms around him. His hand settled between her shoulder blades, then drew her closer. She pressed her face into the place beneath his collar and let out the breath she had been holding.

They stayed there in the quiet hospital lobby, holding each other.
