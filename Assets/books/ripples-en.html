At the Water's Edge

Before

First draft

Aarna

There were two cups on the table and nowhere safe to put either of them.

Aarna had occupied the remaining space with exhibition invitations. She had arranged them in little piles, although they were all identical, and was now moving one from the left-hand pile to the right. In the morning light the paper looked warmer than it had at the printer's. She was trying to decide whether that was good.

Mukund stood in the doorway holding the cups by their handles. "Do the guests get tea, or is the paper having both?"

"The paper has been here longer."

He looked at the table. She looked at him. Then she cleared a rectangle just large enough for his cup.

"And yours?"

"I'm holding it."

"You said that yesterday."

She took the cup from him before he could remind her where yesterday's tea had ended up. He stepped around a canvas leaning against the chair and sat down in the only place she had left him.

Fourteen days. She had counted them before getting out of bed. Now, with the invitations spread between the toast crumbs, the number seemed both generous and impossible. There were labels to check, edges to touch up, people to invite whom she had not told she was painting again until she had something she could show them.

"Read this once more," she said, sliding an invitation towards him.

"I've read it."

"As if you haven't."

Mukund put down his cup. He read the date, the time, the address. Aarna watched his eyes travel over her name.

"Well?"

"Still you."

She laughed, and some small knot behind her ribs loosened. She had seen her name on invoices, parcels, forms. It looked different here, above a small photograph of a painting she had made on the floor of this room.

He held the invitation at arm's length. "Should I wear the blue shirt?"

"You have four blue shirts."

"The one you don't complain about."

"That narrows it to three."

"You wore blue the first time," she said.

"So that's the one."

Three years ago, B(L)IND had put them at the same cafe table. That was the app's arrangement: a blind date with a random person, not a carefully argued case for why two strangers ought to fit. Its name managed to say both blind and bind. Aarna had found that either hopeful or slightly threatening; she had not decided which.

Mukund had arrived early. She had arrived with a streak of paint on her wrist and an apology she abandoned when he asked whether she had been making something. It had been a better first question than what she did for a living.

They had stayed after the cups were cleared. The app had supplied the introduction, but not the second date. They had arranged that themselves.

She let him keep guessing about the shirt. It was easier than telling him how much she liked that he had already thought about what to wear.

Mukund

Mukund's phone vibrated against his leg. A hospital appointment had moved forward, and the person who had moved it had followed the message with two question marks. He turned the screen over before Aarna could see him reading.

He had twenty minutes less than he had thought. He could still sit for five.

Across the table, she had begun rubbing a dry fleck of paint from the side of her thumb. She did that when she was waiting for him to say something more. After three years, he knew the gesture. He did not always know the sentence it was waiting for.

"I like this one," he said, tapping the image on the invitation.

"You said it looked unfinished."

"When it was unfinished."

"It hasn't changed."

He leaned closer. There was a pale rectangle in the painting, a doorway or a window; he had never been certain. He liked that she had made the dark around it look like a place rather than a colour.

"Maybe I have," he said.

She looked up then. He was relieved to discover he had said the right thing without having worked it out first.

When he stood, she caught his wrist. A thin blue mark ran along his cuff. He followed her glance to the chair he had just vacated.

"Don't move," she said.

"That sounds expensive."

She tried the corner of a damp cloth. He watched her bend over the mark with the concentration she usually reserved for a brush. For a moment neither of them spoke.

"Sorry," she said.

"I'll tell them it's a limited edition."

She released his wrist with a small push. At the door he took an invitation from the pile.

"You know where it is," she said.

"It's not for the address."

He put it in the inside pocket of his bag, away from the product brochures, where it would stay flat.

A Pebble

First draft

Aarna

The message arrived while she was deciding where a nail should go.

Aarna had taped a sheet of paper to the living-room wall and drawn a small cross at its centre. She wanted to photograph one painting before it left for the gallery. The light was good there in the afternoon. She had moved a chair, wiped the wall, and found the hammer behind a box of winter clothes.

Her phone lit up on the chair. She put the hammer down to read it.

The gallery's name appeared above a paragraph that began with an apology. She read the first sentence, then the last, looking for the part that would explain the first.

Their funding had fallen through. The exhibition could not proceed. There was no replacement date they could offer.

She read the middle.

Nothing changed.

On the phone, the coordinator sounded as if she had already had this conversation several times. She said the decision had come that morning. She said it was not a judgement on the work.

"But the invitations have gone out," Aarna said.

"I know. I'm sorry."

Aarna looked at the table. Only the spare copies were there; the rest had become messages on other people's phones, a date they might have written down, a reason to ask her later how it had gone.

"All of them," she said, although that was not quite true.

The coordinator went quiet. Aarna could hear typing somewhere at the other end of the call. She wanted to ask a question that would produce a different answer. Instead she asked whether she would receive written confirmation, even though she was holding it.

Afterwards she set the phone face down.

The paper cross waited on the wall. She had measured twice. From the chair to the window, from the floor to the centre of the painting. Everything was ready for one small, ordinary task.

She picked up the hammer, then put it down again.

It was not a judgement on the work. She knew that. The woman had told her plainly, and Aarna had believed her. But belief did not give her anywhere to put the paintings. It did not take back the year she had spent postponing other things, or the money she had counted twice before paying for frames.

She sat on the floor because the chair still had her phone on it. The nearest canvas faced the wall. She turned it around, carefully, and rested it against her knees.

For several minutes she studied a place where one colour met another. Yesterday she had liked that part.

She opened Mukund's messages.

There was a photograph from him, sent just after lunch: the blue mark on his cuff beside a paper cup of coffee. Under it he had written, "Still a limited edition."

She began typing an explanation. Deleted it. Began again.

"The show is canceled. Can you come when you're done?"

His reply came before the screen dimmed.

"Leaving now."

She put the phone beside her, face up this time.

The Ripples Begin

Wave 1

The First Ripple

First draft

Mukund

Mukund knocked once and let himself in when she called. He had spent the drive composing questions, but the sight of her on the floor made him forget their order.

"Hey."

She looked up. There was nothing theatrical about her face. That unsettled him more than tears would have.

He put down his bag and crouched beside her. "Show me what they said."

She handed him the phone. While he read, she leaned against his shoulder, a little awkwardly because he was still balancing on his heels. He lowered one knee to the floor.

Funding. No new date. He read the message again, looking for an opening in its finality. Someone must still have a room. Someone must know someone.

"You don't have to say anything yet," Aarna said.

He thought she meant about the gallery. He placed his hand against her back.

"We'll find another way."

She nodded without lifting her head. He took the nod as permission to continue.

"Two weeks isn't nothing. The work is ready. That's the hard part, and you've already done it."

She moved away enough to take her phone back. He stood to get his laptop.

At work, a canceled appointment came with an immediate choice: spend the next hour resenting it or call the next person. That did not make this the same thing. He knew it was not the same thing. But he needed a first step, something he could place between her and the look she had given him when he walked in.

"Remember that place with the upstairs room?" he asked. "Where we went for the photography thing?"

"I remember."

"They rent it out. I'll check."

He moved the invitations to one side of the table, carefully keeping the piles intact.

Aarna

For the first minute after he arrived, she had felt the beginning of relief.

There had been his shoulder, the familiar smell of his shirt, the fact that he had come before she had worked out how to ask him properly. She had thought she might finally cry.

Now he was at the table, and she was trying to remember whether she had asked him to look for a room.

"They aren't answering," he said. "I'll send a message."

She watched him type. He was quick when he knew what to do. She loved that about him when a train was canceled, when a parcel disappeared, when the frame she had ordered arrived with one corner split. Today his speed made her feel late to something.

"Mukund."

"One second."

She waited. He finished the message and looked at her.

"I don't want to call anyone."

"You don't have to. I'll do it."

"That's not what I meant."

He closed one tab, leaving three others open. She could see him trying to be patient, and she disliked herself for making patience necessary.

He pointed at the canvases. "They're still here. Nothing's happened to the work."

She looked at the painting against her knees. The blue was where she had left it. So was the pale doorway. He was right in a way that made it difficult to tell him why she felt worse.

"I know they're still here."

"Then we haven't lost everything."

She set the painting aside. Its edge made a small, hard sound against the floor.

"Could we not do this now?"

He took his hands off the keyboard.

Mukund

He had left an appointment half-finished. In the car he had turned down a call from his manager. Neither fact seemed worth mentioning, and he knew that mentioning them would turn them into something ugly.

Still, he could feel them collecting behind his teeth.

"I'm only looking," he said.

"I know."

"You asked me to come."

Her face changed. He heard the sentence after he had said it, with a meaning he had not intended.

"Yes," she said. "I did."

The laptop dimmed between them. He touched the trackpad without thinking, and the room brightened with the page he had been reading.

For a moment he could see the two of them reflected in the dark window behind the screen: himself at the table, her on the floor, the empty chair between them.

He moved his hand away.

"Tell me what you want me to do."

Aarna looked at the chair, then at him. He waited for a task.

Wave 2

Crosscurrents

First draft

Aarna

"Sit here," she said.

He looked at the empty chair.

"Here." She touched the floor beside her.

Mukund lowered himself carefully, moving a roll of masking tape out of the way. His knee clicked. On another day she would have teased him for it, and he would have told her that twenty-seven was a difficult age.

She put her hand over his. She could feel him waiting.

That was the difficulty. Even when he was still, she could feel the shape of what he wanted to do next.

"I was going to stand by that painting," she said, pointing towards the window. "At the opening. Not all evening. Just at the beginning, when people came in."

He looked where she pointed.

"I'd worked out where I'd put my bag. Which is stupid."

"It's not stupid."

She waited for him to ask something. What bag, even. Anything that would let the evening she had imagined exist for a moment outside her head.

"You can still have that," he said instead.

She took her hand away.

A year and a half earlier, before a small group show, a frame had split in the taxi. She remembered the noise more distinctly than the journey. A small crack, her immediate certainty that the whole thing was ruined, Mukund asking the driver to pull over beside a hardware shop.

He had returned with glue, two clamps and the wrong sort of tape. They had laughed about the tape while holding the corners together on the pavement. At the gallery she had squeezed his hand and told him she would not have managed without him.

She had meant it. She meant it still.

But there had been something to mend that afternoon.

"Could you stop giving it back to me?" she said.

"Giving what back?"

"The show. Every time I say I've lost it, you tell me I haven't."

He watched her with the expression he wore when a sentence had arrived in a language he almost understood.

Mukund

He wanted to say that he remembered the clamps too.

There were other things he remembered: taking photographs of paintings until she found one with the right light; reading an application three times because the form kept erasing it; sitting in a cafe while she revised an artist statement that seemed to upset her more with every sentence.

He did not think of those things as a debt. He thought of them as evidence that he had once known how to stand beside her.

His phone began vibrating on the table.

"Do you need that?" she asked.

"No."

It stopped, then started again.

He knew who it was. He also knew that ignoring it would make the next morning harder. He could hear himself becoming short with her before he had said anything.

"I need two minutes," he admitted.

She nodded. He went to the kitchen, although the doorway did very little to make it another room.

He explained the unfinished appointment. Not all of it; just enough. He would send the revised figures before nine tomorrow. No, the hospital had not withdrawn. Yes, he understood.

When he returned, Aarna was gathering the invitations.

"I wasn't leaving," he said.

"I know."

She had put them in one uneven pile. That worried him more than the earlier silence.

"You don't have to put them away."

"I want the table back."

He reached for the laptop and stopped himself. "Okay."

He carried the cups into the kitchen. One was almost full. Tea had dried in a brown line inside the other.

Over the sink, he remembered a late evening in their second year together. He had missed his quarterly target and told Aarna he was fine. She had ordered dinner, put her feet in his lap, and kept talking about a film. He had been grateful not to explain.

Perhaps she had believed him. He had never considered that possibility. He had counted her silence as understanding and then expected her to recognise his silence as pain.

Aarna

At six, the light left the square of paper on the wall.

She could hear water running in the kitchen. He was washing cups because they were there and could be washed. She knew that habit. Sometimes it was the gentlest thing about him.

She wanted to go to him. She also wanted him to come back without having to be called.

When he did, he brought a glass of water. She accepted it.

"Have you eaten?" he asked.

"Not really."

He nodded towards his phone. "I can order something."

"Later."

He sat in the chair this time. Neither of them mentioned it.

"You have to stay strong, Aarna," he said quietly. "Don't let one afternoon take all of it."

She turned the glass between her palms.

He had meant to place something solid under her feet. What she heard was that the floor she was sitting on was somewhere she ought to have left already.

Wave 3

The Crest

First draft

Aarna

"What happens if I don't?" she asked.

"Don't what?"

"Stay strong."

Mukund looked tired. She knew she looked tired too. It made each of them seem unreasonable to the other before they had finished speaking.

"That's not what I meant."

"Then what did you mean?"

"That this isn't the end."

"I haven't said it's the end."

"You're talking as if everything's gone."

She stood too quickly. The glass struck the edge of the table, spilling water over the top invitation. She caught it upright. For a moment they both watched the paper darken.

Mukund reached for a cloth.

"Leave it."

"It's water."

"I know what it is."

He set the cloth down beside the glass. Her own voice had startled her. She could have said sorry then, and wanted to, but something else was already rising behind it.

"I have spent the whole afternoon trying not to make this too much for you."

"For me?"

"Trying to explain it properly. Trying to say it in a way you can do something with."

"I came as soon as you asked."

"I know. You're here. But I keep feeling like I have to go somewhere else to be upset."

He sat back. She saw that she had hurt him, and for one small, shameful instant was glad that something she had said had finally reached him.

"You don't get to tell me I don't care," he said.

"I didn't say that."

"You didn't have to."

Mukund

He had no idea where to put his hands. He folded them, unfolded them, moved the damp invitation away from the dry ones.

He wanted to explain the drive, the appointment, the calls he had not taken. He wanted to explain that another venue had not been an insult but the only useful thought he had managed to have.

"I can't make the gallery change its mind," he said.

"I'm not asking you to."

"Then stop looking at me as if I've failed you."

The words made the room smaller.

Aarna lowered her eyes. He wished she would answer quickly, angrily even. He did not know how to bear the pause.

"This isn't a test," she said.

"It feels like one."

In their first year together he had believed being dependable would be enough. He would arrive when he said he would. He would remember what she needed from the shop. If she called late, he would answer. These were things he could promise without lying.

The other things had no clear edges. When to speak, when to stop. Whether silence meant stay with me or leave me alone. He was not proud of how often he waited for a practical problem to rescue him from guessing.

"When you hurt like this," he said, "I don't know how to be useful."

"I don't need you to be useful all the time."

He almost asked what she needed him for, then. He heard how it would sound and did not say it.

Instead he looked at the open laptop. A room hire page had become a dark rectangle. His first impulse was to wake it.

Aarna

She sat in the empty chair.

Now the table was between them, and she could see his cuff properly. The blue mark had survived the entire day.

"I wanted you to be sad with me," she said.

"I am sad."

"I can't tell."

He let out a breath that was almost a laugh but had no amusement in it. "I can't tell what counts."

That stopped her.

She had wanted the right response to prove that he understood. She had not told him what it would look like, because having to tell him had seemed like proof that he did not.

Knowing that did not make his words about strength hurt less.

"When I come to you for help," she said, more slowly, "don't tell me to be strong. I came to you to be weak for a while."

He rubbed his palms against his trousers.

"I thought if I let you believe it was all gone, I'd be making it worse."

"You don't have to believe it. I don't even believe it all the time. I just need to be allowed to feel it before someone explains why I shouldn't."

He looked at her then.

"I can want to help," he said, "and still not know how to help you the way you need."

There it was, without a promise attached. She did not know whether to be relieved by the honesty or frightened by how little it offered her.

His phone lit up. Neither of them looked.

"I need a minute," he said. "Not to leave. Just a minute without trying to say the right thing."

She nodded. When he went to the kitchen, she did not follow him.

The wet invitation lay on the cloth. Her name was still readable.

Wave 4

The Trough

First draft

Mukund

The kitchen light was brighter than he wanted.

Mukund stood under it with both hands on the counter. There was no reason to turn on the tap. He had already washed everything.

He thought about the question he had nearly asked: what she needed him for, if not to be useful. In the kitchen it sounded worse, and also more honest.

He had never liked waiting beside something he could not change. If a call went badly, he made another. If he felt embarrassed, he prepared better for next time. Even rest became easier when he could tell himself it would make tomorrow more productive.

He had carried that habit into the room and handed it to her as comfort.

His phone vibrated again. This time it was the venue. A room might be available the following weekend. They wanted dates, numbers, a deposit.

He read the message twice. Here was a way to return with something better than an apology.

He put the phone in his pocket.

When he came back, Aarna was standing by the wall, holding the little sheet with the cross on it.

"I was going to photograph it," she said.

"The one by the window?"

She nodded.

"You can still..." He stopped.

She looked at him. For a second he wanted to pretend he had meant another sentence.

"I'm doing it again," he said.

"A little."

He nodded. The correction did not feel good, but nothing terrible happened after it.

"I'm sorry I made you defend being upset."

Aarna looked down at the paper. He did not add anything.

Aarna

She had been preparing an answer to a sentence he had not finished. Without it, she had nothing ready.

The cross was slightly off centre. She folded the paper once, following a crease the tape had left, then opened it again.

"I'm sorry I spoke as if coming here didn't matter," she said. "It did."

He leaned against the wall, leaving space between them.

"And I'm sorry about the cloth."

"It survived."

She laughed once. It surprised them both.

For the first time since the call, she noticed how hungry she was. The feeling was inconveniently ordinary. She had been devastated all afternoon, and her body still expected dinner.

"Can we eat something?" she asked.

He reached for his phone, then looked at her before unlocking it.

"Food," she said. "You can solve food."

His smile was small and exhausted.

While they waited, they sat on the floor again. He chose the place beside her without asking which one.

"What was it going to be like?" he asked.

"What?"

"The first evening."

She could not answer immediately. Earlier she had wanted the question so badly that hearing it now made her angry for a moment, as if it had arrived after a door had closed.

Then she told him about the bag.

She told him about the black shoes she had bought and not broken in, and the possibility that nobody would come until half an hour after the opening time. She had imagined him pretending not to watch the entrance. She had imagined a stranger stopping in front of a painting and staying there.

"Not buying it," she said. "Just looking long enough that I could stop wondering if there was anything to see."

Mukund looked at the canvases around them.

"You wanted somebody to meet the work."

She nodded. It was not everything, but it was close enough that she did not have to correct him.

The tears came then. Quietly at first, and then with the ugly effort of trying to keep them quiet. She covered her face.

"Can I?" he asked, touching her elbow.

She leaned towards him.

Mukund

He put an arm around her and discovered how much effort it took not to speak.

Several sentences offered themselves. The work was good. There would be another show. This would look different in a month.

He believed all of them. He let them wait.

At one point she apologised for getting his shirt wet.

"It's already painted," he said.

Her laugh broke in the middle. He held her while it turned back into crying.

He was not calm. His leg had gone numb, his phone was vibrating in his pocket, and some part of him still wanted a timetable for when she would feel better. But he could have those feelings without putting them in her hands.

When the doorbell rang, he waited until she moved away.

"I'll get it," he said.

This time it was only the door.

Wave 5

Still Water

First draft

Aarna

They ate at the table with the invitations stacked on the windowsill.

The food had cooled while they looked for plates. Aarna ate anyway, surprised by how quickly hunger became hunger again once she began. Mukund gave her the last piece of bread without making a ceremony of it.

Nothing had happened to the gallery. The message on her phone remained exactly as it had been in the afternoon.

Halfway through dinner, she looked towards the paintings and stopped chewing.

He noticed. "Back again?"

She nodded.

"Yeah," he said.

It was not an impressive answer. She was grateful for it.

Later, while he folded the empty paper bags, she picked up the damp invitation. One corner had curled. She pressed it flat with her palm, knowing it would rise again.

"I might need help tomorrow," she said.

"With the gallery?"

"Telling people. Asking about the money. I don't know all of it yet."

"We can make a list."

She looked up.

"Tomorrow," he added.

She put the invitation down. "Tomorrow."

It mattered that the word could mean both not now and not never.

Mukund

At eleven, he told her about the message from the other venue.

He did not tell her the price or the date. He said only that someone had replied, and asked whether she wanted to hear about it.

"Not tonight."

"Okay."

The answer was easy to say. Accepting it took another second.

He closed the laptop properly this time and put it in his bag.

"I won't always catch it," he said.

Aarna was sitting on the edge of the sofa, feet tucked beneath her. "What?"

"When I'm trying to fix something you haven't asked me to fix."

She looked at the bag, then back at him.

"I don't want us to have this whole day every time."

"Neither do I."

He sat beside her. There was room for two people if he moved the cushion. He moved it.

"Could you tell me?" he asked. "Before we get this far. Just say that you need me to stay with you, not make a plan."

"I can try. But you have to hear it when I do."

"I know."

"And sometimes I won't know what I need."

He rested his hands on his knees. That was the part for which he would have liked instructions.

"Then I'll ask," he said. "And if you don't know, I can sit for a while."

She considered him for long enough that he wanted to improve the promise. He left it as it was.

"You can tell me when you don't know, too," she said. "You don't have to turn it into a solution so I won't notice."

He looked at her.

"I was scared this afternoon," he said.

She took his hand without telling him he had not needed to be.

Aarna

The invitation in his bag slid out when he went to fetch his charger.

Aarna picked it up before he could. It was flat and clean, the corner protected inside a folded product sheet. Her name looked back at her from the middle of it.

"You kept it."

"I took it this morning."

"I know."

She held it for another moment, then gave it back.

He did not say that it would be useful for the next exhibition. He put it where it had been.

At eleven forty-two, she switched off the bright light above the table. Only the lamp beside the sofa remained. The room had stopped resembling somewhere she had failed to get ready.

The paper with the cross was still on the wall. The hammer was still on the chair. She could leave them there until morning.

"Stay?" she said.

"Yes."

She made room for him, and when he sat down she rested her head against his shoulder. Neither of them was particularly comfortable. His arm was at the wrong angle; her hair caught under his collar. They adjusted.

After a while, he asked whether she wanted the lamp off.

"Not yet."

He left it on.

Across the room, the pale doorway in her painting was just visible. She looked at it until her eyes grew tired. She did not have to decide tonight whether it was finished.
